The four families

Almost every perfume belongs mostly to one family. Knowing which ones you are drawn to is the quickest way to narrow a shelf of bottles down to three.

Oud

The resin-darkened heartwood of the agarwood tree, prized across South Asia and the Gulf for centuries. Smoky, leathery and faintly sweet, it is usually paired with rose, saffron or amber, and it is the richest and longest-lasting family we carry.

Typical notes
Oud, saffron, rose, amber, sandalwood
Wear it for
Weddings, Eid, winter evenings
Shop oud fragrances

Woody

Cedar, vetiver, sandalwood and leather: dry, grounded and quietly confident. Woody scents sit close to the skin and change slowly through the day, so they are easy to wear to work and still interesting at dinner.

Typical notes
Cedar, vetiver, leather, birch, patchouli
Wear it for
The office, dinners, the cooler months
Shop woody fragrances

Floral

Rose, jasmine, peony and iris, from dewy and just-picked to deep and velvety. Modern florals are rarely sugary: a little citrus lifts them and soft musk keeps them close.

Typical notes
Rose, jasmine, peony, iris, white musk
Wear it for
Daytime, spring and summer weddings, gifts
Shop floral fragrances

Fresh

Citrus, sea air and green notes: bright, clean and easy in the heat. They fade sooner than the other families, which is why a fresh scent rewards a second spray after lunch.

Typical notes
Bergamot, grapefruit, sea salt, neroli, vetiver
Wear it for
Summer days, travel, the gym bag

How a perfume unfolds

A perfume is not one smell but three, arriving in turn as the lightest molecules evaporate and the heavier ones take over. That is why a scent on a paper strip tells you so little.

Top notes first 10 to 15 minutes
The bright first impression: citrus, light spice, herbs. Lovely, but gone before you leave the house.
Heart notes the next few hours
The character of the perfume: florals, spices, the smoothed-out middle of oud. This is what people around you notice.
Base notes hours, sometimes into the next day
Woods, resins, amber and musk. They anchor everything above and are what you smell on a scarf the morning after.

So test on skin, and wait at least twenty minutes before you decide.

What the name on the bottle means

The words after the perfume's name tell you roughly how much perfume oil it contains. The figures below are general industry ranges, typically quoted across the trade; each house sets its own.

Perfume concentrations, typical oil content and typical wear
Concentration Perfume oil, typically Typical wear on skin Feels like
Eau de Toilette EDT 5–15% 3 to 5 hours Light and airy, easy to reapply
Eau de Parfum EDP 15–20% 5 to 8 hours Fuller, carries through a working day
Parfum or Extrait 20–30% 8 hours or more Dense and rich; a dab goes a long way
Attar or perfume oil Pure oil, no alcohol Often all day Sits close to the skin, warms slowly

Concentration is only half the story. A citrus Parfum can fade sooner than a woody Eau de Toilette, because fresh notes evaporate faster whatever the strength.

Wearing it well

Longevity and sillage

Longevity is how long a fragrance lasts on you. Sillage, from the French for the wake behind a boat, is the trail it leaves in a room. They are not the same thing: an attar can last all day while staying so close that only someone you embrace will notice it, and a bright citrus can fill a lift for ten minutes and then vanish.

Dry skin holds scent less well than moisturised skin, heat makes a perfume project further but fade sooner, and you stop noticing your own fragrance within the hour even when everyone else still can.

How to apply

  1. Start with clean, moisturised skin; an unscented lotion gives the oils something to hold.
  2. Spray from about a hand's length away so the mist spreads instead of pooling.
  3. Choose pulse points, where the skin is warm: wrists, the sides of the neck, inside the elbows.
  4. Do not rub your wrists together. It crushes the top notes and rushes the perfume to its base.
  5. Two or three sprays are enough for a fragrance you wear all day; one for an attar.

Through the seasons

In the heat, reach for fresh and floral scents and use a little less: warmth makes every perfume louder. Keep citrus-heavy fragrances off skin that will sit in strong sun, and spray your clothes instead.

In the cold, oud, amber and woods come into their own. Cold air flattens light notes, so a richer scent and an extra spray on a shawl or coat collar will carry through a long evening.

Storage

Heat, light and air are what age a perfume. Keep bottles capped, in their boxes, somewhere cool and dark: a bedroom drawer, not the bathroom shelf and never the car. Stored well, an opened bottle usually stays at its best for three to five years, and a sealed one for longer.

If a fragrance starts to smell sharp or sour at the first spray, its top notes have turned.
